How to Reduce Image Size Without Losing Quality

Guide • Updated: 2026-11-24 • Free, in your browser — your photos are never uploaded

Most people shrink an image the wrong way round: they drag a quality slider down until the file is small enough, and then wonder why the photo looks soft and blotchy. There is a better order, and it works for almost every photo — a phone picture for a job form, a product shot for a listing, or a picture for a website. Resize the dimensions first, pick the right format, and only then compress to an exact KB target. Done in that order, a photo can lose most of its file size and still look the same on screen.

This guide explains each step, what quality loss actually looks like, and when to stop shrinking. The ideas here are general technique, tied to how the tools work — no magic settings, and no invented numbers. If you are shrinking a photo for an exam or visa form, remember: rules change between cycles — always confirm against the official notification or portal before you submit.

Quick facts — the order that works

  • 1. Resize dimensions first — shrinking pixels is the biggest win and costs no visible quality
  • 2. Pick the right format — JPG for photos, PNG only for graphics and text, WebP usually smaller than JPG
  • 3. Compress to an exact KB target — the compressor searches quality and size until the file lands at or under your target
  • 4. Shrink in steps for big reductions — stepwise downscaling keeps edges cleaner than one huge jump
  • 5. Always compress from the original — never re-compress a JPG many times; each save softens it a little
  • 6. Keep the original file — the tool only downloads a new copy, so your original stays untouched
  • 7. Check at 100% zoom — check the result at full size before you submit it anywhere

1. Resize dimensions first — the biggest win

A modern phone photo is often around 4032 px wide. If that photo will be shown at 1200 px wide — on a form, a listing or a web page — then most of its pixels will never be seen. Those unseen pixels still take up bytes, so a 4032 px photo shown at 1200 px wastes most of its file size for nothing. Shrinking the dimensions to the size the photo will actually be used at is the single biggest win, and it costs no visible quality, because the pixels you remove were never going to be displayed. Before you touch any quality setting, ask: how wide will this photo actually be shown? Resize to that width, and only then think about compression. A photo resized to its real display size is often already small enough without any heavy compression at all.

2. Pick the right format — JPG, PNG or WebP

Format choice matters more than most people think, because each format is built for a different kind of image. Use JPG for photos: it is designed for the smooth tones and gradients of a photograph, and it compresses them well. Use PNG only for graphics, text or screenshots with sharp edges — logos, diagrams and screenshots with crisp lines — where JPG would smear the edges. For those sharp-edged images, PNG keeps the lines clean. Use WebP where it is accepted: WebP is usually smaller than JPG for the same photo quality, so if the form or site you are uploading to accepts WebP, it is often the smallest option. But do not convert a photo to PNG to make it smaller — a photo saved as PNG is usually much larger than the same photo as JPG, because PNG is not built for photographic detail. Check what the destination accepts first, then choose the format that fits the image.

3. Use an exact KB target instead of guessing a quality slider

Guessing a quality slider is slow and unreliable: you pick 70%, download the file, find it is still too big, pick 50%, download again, and repeat until you give up. An exact KB target is faster and more accurate. Tell the compressor the size you need — say, 20 KB or 200 KB — and it searches quality and size until the file lands at or under your target. That is how ResizeHub’s compressor works: you set the target, and it does the searching for you. This is especially useful for exam and job forms, which usually state a KB limit rather than a quality percentage. Set the limit as the target, let the tool find the best quality that fits inside it, and check the result before you submit it.

4. Shrink in steps for big reductions

If you need to take a photo from very large to very small — a full-size phone photo down to a small thumbnail, for example — shrink it in steps rather than in one huge jump. Stepwise downscaling keeps edges cleaner than one huge jump, because each step has less work to do and less detail to throw away at once. In practice, that means resizing to an intermediate size first, then to the final size, rather than going straight from the original to the smallest size in a single move. You will not always need this for a modest reduction, but for a big one, the difference is visible: edges stay crisper and fine detail holds up better.

5. Never re-compress a JPG many times

Every time a JPG is saved, it softens a little. The first save from the original is fine; the fifth save of a save of a save is not. Each round of compression throws away a little more detail, and the damage adds up — edges go soft, flat areas get blotchy, and fine texture disappears. So always compress from the original file, never from a copy you already compressed. Keep the original safe on your device, and if a compressed version does not look right, go back to the original and make a fresh one with a different target. The tool only downloads a new copy, so your original file is never changed by using it — but that only helps if you still have the original. Do not delete it after the first compression.

What quality loss actually looks like

Quality loss is not one thing — it shows up in different ways, and knowing what to look for helps you judge a result. Soft edges are the most common sign: hair, fabric and text lose their crispness and look slightly out of focus. Blocky flat areas are another: a plain wall or a clear sky breaks into visible squares or patches instead of a smooth tone. Colour banding in skies is a third: a smooth gradient from light to dark blue turns into visible stripes, because the compression could not keep the subtle steps between shades. And faces are the most sensitive of all: skin that was smooth becomes waxy and plastic-looking when it has been compressed too hard. Check the result at 100% zoom before you submit it anywhere — at thumbnail size, all of these problems hide, and you will only see them when the photo is viewed at full size.

When to stop shrinking

There is a point where no amount of clever technique will make a photo smaller without visible damage, and recognising it saves you from submitting a bad file. If text becomes unreadable, faces turn waxy, edges go soft, or flat areas turn blocky, the target is too small for that photo. The fix is not more compression — it is a bigger target or a smaller dimension. Try a slightly larger KB target first: a small increase in file size often restores a lot of quality. Or reduce the dimensions a little more, so the same KB budget is spread over fewer pixels. If neither helps, the photo itself may be the problem — a blurry or noisy original will not survive much compression at all, and starting from a sharper original is the only real fix. A good rule is simple: if you can see the damage at 100% zoom, the person checking your form will see it too.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Dragging the quality slider first — resize dimensions before you compress, or you are throwing away quality you did not need to lose.
  • Saving a photo as PNG — PNG is for graphics and text, not photos; a photo as PNG is usually much larger than as JPG.
  • Re-compressing a compressed JPG — always go back to the original for a fresh copy.
  • Deleting the original — keep it, so you can redo the file if a limit changes or a result looks bad.
  • Judging quality at thumbnail size — check at 100% zoom, where soft edges, banding and blockiness actually show.
  • Chasing a KB target that is too small — if the photo falls apart, use a bigger target or a smaller dimension instead of more compression.
